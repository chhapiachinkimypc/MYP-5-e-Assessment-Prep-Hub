Step 1 — Origin: Key Questions to Ask
  • → Is it a primary (original, first-hand) or secondary (interpreted, hindsight) source?
  • → Where does this source come from? When was it published?
  • → Who is the author, publisher or organisation?
  • → Are the author's credentials or affiliations given? Are they reliable?
Step 2 — Purpose: Key Questions to Ask
  • → What was the purpose of the information? Is it fact, fiction, opinion or propaganda?
  • → Who is the intended audience? Does the point of view appear objective or impartial?
  • → Why did the author create it in this way? Can it tell you more than is on the surface?
Step 3 — Value: Key Questions to Ask
  • → How has the source helped with your research? What questions has it answered?
  • → What can we tell about the time period, debates or the author's opinion from the source?
  • → Can you verify the information in another source (corroboration)?
Step 4 — Limitations: Key Questions to Ask
  • → At what point does this source stop being of value? What part of the story can we NOT tell from it?
  • → What does the author leave out and why (if you know)?
  • → Being biased does NOT limit the value of a source by itself — you must explain specifically: who is it biased towards/against, and what part of the story is MISSING because of parts left out?
  • → Does it inaccurately reflect anything about the time period?
🎯 Marking Reminder (from Assessment Rubric)
To score in the top band, your OPVL response must offer two or more insights that are accurate and relevant to both the value and the limitation of the document — connected clearly to its origin and purpose.

The Thinking Critically task (36 marks, including 20 for Criterion D) most commonly uses "Discuss" as the command term. Write in essay form — no bullet points within the essay.
📖 What is a Discussion Essay?
Many essay titles require you to examine both sides of a situation and to conclude by saying which side you favour. These are known as discussion or for and against essays. In this sense, the academic meaning of the word discuss is similar to its everyday meaning — two people talking about a topic from different sides. For a discussion essay, a balanced view is normally essential.

This makes discussion essays distinct from persuasion essays, for which only one side of the argument is given. When writing a discussion essay, it is important to ensure that facts and opinions are clearly separated. Often you will examine what other people have already said on the same subject and include this information using paraphrasing and summarising skills, as well as correct citations.

Discussion Essay — Structure Guide

Always write in flowing prose. Every claim needs specific evidence or an example.

Para 1
INTRO

General Statement + Thesis Statement

  • General Statement: Introduce the topic and give background information. The background for a short essay will generally be one or two sentences. (Draw attention to the issue)
  • Thesis Statement: The most important part of the introduction. It gives the reader clear information about the content of the essay and helps them understand it more easily. (Make it specific — state your position clearly)
Para 2
POINT 1

Topic Statement (Topic + Controlling Idea)

  • Point 1 — State your first argument clearly
  • Explanation — Develop the point with reasoning
  • Evidence / Example — Support with specific facts, data or examples
Para 3
POINT 2

Topic Statement (Topic + Controlling Idea)

  • Point 2 — State your second argument
  • Explanation — Develop the point with reasoning
  • Evidence / Example — Support with specific facts, data or examples
Para 4
POINT 3

Topic Statement (Topic + Controlling Idea)

  • Point 3 — State your third argument (this can be the counter-argument)
  • Explanation — Develop the point
  • Evidence / Example — Support with specific facts, data or examples
Conclusion
SUMMARY

Summary + Final Comment / Appraisal

  • Summary — Briefly restate the main points made in the essay without introducing new points
  • Final comment / appraisal — State your clear judgement and position
  • Useful transition signals for the concluding sentence:
    In conclusion… · Thus… · In short… · In summary… · In brief… · Therefore… · These examples show that… · This evidence strongly suggests that…
⚡ Quick Essentials
  • The most common command terms are "Discuss", "To What Extent", Evaluate, Explain
  • You may or may not be given a source — be prepared for both
  • Always write in flowing prose — never use bullet points within your essay
  • Clearly separate facts from opinions throughout
  • Use paraphrasing, summarising skills and correct citations when referring to sources
  • ✍️
    The conclusion MUST answer the question directly. State a clear degree or position: "In conclusion…", "This evidence strongly suggests that…". Avoid vague endings.
  • 📄
    Always cite sources specifically. "As Source A (a 2019 newspaper article) argues…" is better than "according to the sources." Name the source, its type and date.
  • 🌍
    Use named case studies. "In Bangladesh, the garment industry employed 4 million workers due to globalisation" scores higher than "in some developing countries." Specific > vague.

Step 1 — Origin
  • → Primary (first-hand) or secondary (interpreted) source?
  • → Where and when was it created? Who is the author or organisation?
  • → Are the author's credentials reliable?
Step 2 — Purpose
  • → What was the purpose? Fact, fiction, opinion or propaganda?
  • → Who is the intended audience? Objective or impartial?
  • → What does it say — and what can it tell you beyond the surface?
Step 3 — Value
  • → How has the source helped your research? What questions has it answered?
  • → Can the information be verified through another source (corroboration)?
Step 4 — Limitations
  • → At what point does this source stop being of value?
  • → What part of the story can we NOT tell from this document?
  • → Bias alone does not limit value — specify who it is biased towards/against and what is MISSING.
  • → Does it inaccurately reflect the time period?

📖 What is a Discussion Essay?
Many essay titles require you to examine both sides of a situation and to conclude by saying which side you favour. These are known as discussion or for and against essays. A balanced view is normally essential. This makes discussion essays distinct from persuasion essays, for which only one side is given. Facts and opinions must be clearly separated throughout.
